    Section 1 · The Why

    College credit should count — wherever a student goes.

    Most students don't walk a straight line from starting college to finishing it. They may start college while in high school. They may transfer. They may change majors. They may start at one institution and graduate from another. What really matters is whether the general education credits they've already earned build competency in broad academic areas, prepare them for continuing success in college, and travel with them if they decide to transfer to another public institution in Kentucky. Ultimately, students and their advisors need to understand how to maximize transferability and the value of credits earned.

    A brief introduction from CPE on why the Gen Ed Core matters

    This training is for the adults who help students figure out their next best step toward a college degree or credential. The high school counselor sorting out which early college classes are most helpful to individual students. The college advisor looking at a transcript from a transfer student. The teacher of a student who just changed their major — for the third time. Your job isn't to memorize policy. It's to know enough about how Kentucky's college credit system works that the next student you talk to walks away with a clearer, more beneficial plan than they walked in with.

    Before we get into how the system works, a quick reality-check.

    What percentage of college students transfer or change their major at least once?
    The data
    About 38% transfer at least once. About one in three change their major.

    According to the National Student Clearinghouse, about 38% of students who start college will move to a different school at least once within six years. Separately, federal data shows about one in three bachelor's-degree students change their major within their first three years. This isn't unusual — it's how most students actually move through college.

    National Student Clearinghouse Research Center, Transfer & Mobility · NCES, Beginning Postsecondary Students Longitudinal Study (BPS:12/14)

    When students move — between schools, between majors, sometimes both — the general education credits they've already earned should be an obvious asset and never a liability. That's the challenge Kentucky's Statewide General Education Core is designed to address. The next ten sections walk through what it is, how it works, and how you can use it in your conversations to help students move toward a college degree or credential.

    📋
    By the end of this training You'll be able to explain what the Gen Ed Core is and how it lets credits move between Kentucky's public colleges. You'll know the five categories and what kinds of classes count for each one. You'll be able to read the three levels of completion that show up on a college transcript. You'll see how the Gen Ed Core makes dual credit a smart choice for a lot of students. And you'll work through two real student situations to tie it all together.
    Section 2 · The Framework

    Twenty-four colleges. One promise. Credits that travel.

    Kentucky's Statewide General Education Core is detailed in the state's Transfer Policy. The agreement among the state's eight 4-year and sixteen 2-year public colleges and universities stipulates that courses identified as general education by one public institution may be transferred and will count as general education requirements if a student moves from one public postsecondary institution to another.

    This isn't a handshake deal — it's required by Kentucky law. The current version of the Gen Ed Core has been in effect since the fall of 2012, and the rules apply to every public college and university in the state.

    Twenty-four public colleges and universities take part:

    The 8 four-year universities
    EKU KSU Morehead State Murray State NKU UK UofL WKU
    The 16 KCTCS community and technical colleges
    Ashland Big Sandy Bluegrass Elizabethtown Gateway Hazard Henderson Hopkinsville Jefferson Madisonville Maysville Owensboro Somerset Southcentral Kentucky Southeast Kentucky West Kentucky

    More about the agreement. First, the general education core is organized into five subject categories: communication (writing and speaking), math and statistics, sciences, social sciences (like psychology, sociology, and history), and arts and humanities. Second, each category has a set of student learning outcomes or the skills students should walk away with from classes in each category. Third — and this is the important one — the policy guarantees that if a student takes a class at one of Kentucky's public colleges that meets those skills and is designated by the institution as general education, all the others will count it as if the student had taken the general education course at their institution.

    The important part to know: the agreement gives attention to what students learn, not to what the class is called. A receiving school isn't checking whether the sending school's "ENG 101" lines up with its own "ENG 101." Rather, it's checking whether the student learning outcomes ensure that the student walks out with the writing skills the policy expects and that are needed to be successful as a college student. That's how general education transfer operates smoothly across all twenty-four public institutions.

    📜
    The guarantee, in one sentence If a student finishes the Gen Ed Core at any of these twenty-four colleges, every other school in the agreement will accept it as having met their general education requirements too. The new school might still have a few of its own extras to add on top — but the core travels.
    Section 3 · The Categories

    Five categories. What's in each one?

    Before we dive into what's in each category, a quick word about why having five matters in the first place.

    But first — why five categories?

    Students sometimes ask straight out: "Why do I have to take art history if I'm going to be a nurse?" The truth is that no one's life or career stays neatly inside one category. A nurse who never thought through ethics may struggle when faced with difficult medical decisions or inexplicable tragedy in clinical settings. An engineer who never learned to write clearly will struggle to lead a team. A teacher who never studied history will have a hard time making sense of what's happening in their own classroom.

    The five categories exist so students get exposed to different ways of thinking before they pick one path — and meet ideas, perspectives, and people they may never have gone looking for on their own. When students take courses across the five categories of general education, they build foundational knowledge in broad academic topics and develop skills and competencies necessary to succeed through their postsecondary program. A student sure they wanted to be an accountant takes Intro to Psychology and gets fascinated by how people make decisions. A student certain about engineering takes a literature class and realizes they're a better writer than they thought. A student in early childhood education takes a science class and discovers a love for biology that changes how they'll teach. Those moments matter. They shape majors, careers, and the kind of adult a student turns into.

    So when a student asks you about all this, here's what's worth saying: the Gen Ed Core isn't a tax on their time. It's the part of college where they get to explore new ideas and topics and build communication and thinking skills that will be useful for their whole lives.

    Now — the five categories

    Click any card to open it up. You'll see what kinds of classes may count, what students walk away knowing, the code that designates the general education category and shows up on a transcript, and how many credits the category needs.

    What students learn
    How to write clearly for different audiences, how to research and cite sources, how to listen and speak with confidence in front of a group, and how to build a fair argument.
    Sample classes
    English Composition I English Composition II Public Speaking Business Communication
    The codes
    WC for written communication, OC for oral communication. Most students take at least one of each.
    Why it matters
    Every career relies on writing and speaking — from nurses charting patient notes to engineers explaining a design to a client. This is the most universal category.
    What students learn
    How to read information shown in numbers, charts, and graphs. How to do the math a problem actually needs. How to spot when an answer looks off and ask why.
    Sample classes
    College Algebra Statistics Quantitative Reasoning Finite Math
    The code
    QR. Students usually finish this category with one course.
    Why it matters
    Healthcare runs on math (dosages, vitals, lab values). So do business, technology, engineering, and any career that involves a budget — which is most of them.
    What students learn
    How scientists actually answer questions — designing experiments, collecting data, making predictions, and connecting science to real issues people care about.
    Sample classes
    Biology with Lab Chemistry with Lab Physics with Lab Geology with Lab Astronomy with Lab
    The codes
    NS for natural sciences. SL marks the required hands-on lab. Every student needs lab-based science somewhere in this category.
    Why it matters
    Nursing, pre-med, engineering, agriculture, environmental science, and teaching all build on the science a student does here.
    What students learn
    How researchers study human behavior, communities, and culture. How to apply that to real problems — and how to talk and write about people fairly and accurately.
    Sample classes
    Intro to Psychology Sociology Anthropology Economics Political Science
    The code
    SB. Students need at least two classes here, and they must come from different subjects (you can't do two psychology classes and call it done).
    Why it matters
    Teaching, social work, healthcare, business, law, and government all start with understanding how people and groups work.
    What students learn
    How human creativity and experience — art, music, literature — reflects and shapes the culture around it. How to tell strong arguments and reliable sources from weak ones. How to think critically about big questions and challenges that people have faced in the past and today. The tools, methods, and special terms used in different arts and humanities subjects.
    Sample classes
    U.S. History World Literature Art Appreciation Music Appreciation Philosophy
    The code
    AH. Students need at least two classes here.
    Why it matters
    Communications, education, design, law, ministry, public service — careers that depend on thinking critically and writing well — are built on this foundation.
    💡
    Pro tip on transcript codes Those two-letter codes (WC, OC, QR, NS, SL, SB, AH) are the same on every Kentucky public college transcript. Once a student has them next to a class, that class counts in the general education core at public colleges and universities statewide.
    Section 4 · How Transfer Works

    Every class counts on its own.

    Most students don't wait until they've finished a whole category — much less the entire Gen Ed Core — before they move or graduate. The Transfer Policy is built for that reality. Here's how it works.

    The building block of the Gen Ed Core is the individual class. If a Kentucky public college has designated a class as general education — meaning the transcript shows one of the codes (WC, OC, QR, NS, SL, SB, or AH) next to it — that single class transfers to every other Kentucky public college as general education.

    It doesn't matter whether the student has taken one class or twenty. Each class moves on its own, into the same category at the new school. This is what course-by-course transfer means, and it's the most common transfer story in the state.

    💡
    What this means in practice A student can take a single dual credit class at a KCTCS campus in eleventh grade, enroll at KCTCS after graduation, and then move to UK two years later — and every one of those individual Gen Ed Core classes follows them the whole way. Each one lands in its correct category at each new school.

    Three levels of completion

    When students take enough Gen Ed Core classes to reach certain milestones, those milestones show up on their college transcript at one of three levels of completion. Knowing which level a student has reached tells you exactly how much of their general education will move with them to a new school.

    1
    Level One
    Category Certified

    The student has finished some categories — at least one, but not all five. The categories they finished will transfer. They'll need to complete the rest at their new school.

    2
    Level Two
    Core Certified

    The student has finished all five categories — at least 30 credit hours total, with no class counted twice. The whole Gen Ed Core transfers. The new school can only ask them to add its own specific extras, if it has any.

    3
    Level Three
    Fully Certified

    The student has finished the Gen Ed Core plus any extra general education classes their school required on top. Their full general education program moves with them — nothing left to repeat.

    💡
    Why this matters when you're talking to students Two transcripts can look almost identical at first glance and mean completely different things for transfer. A student who's "Category Certified" in three out of five categories has a very different next step than one who's "Core Certified." If you only have time to look at one thing on a transfer transcript, look at the level of completion.

    One last piece of good news. If a student graduates from a KCTCS school (a community or technical college) with an Associate's degree (AA or AS) through one of the approved transfer programs, every Kentucky public university treats them as having finished all general education requirements — and they enter the new school as juniors, not freshmen. The Gen Ed Core is the foundation that makes that promise possible.

    Section 5 · Knowledge Check

    Quick check: where does each class belong?

    Five common college classes. For each one, pick the Gen Ed Core category it belongs to. Pick carefully — you can't change your answer.

    Question 1 of 5
    Where does Public Speaking fit?
    Public Speaking is a classic Communication class — specifically the oral communication (OC) side of the category.
    Question 2 of 5
    Where does General Psychology fit?
    Psychology studies how people think and behave — that puts it in Social & Behavioral Sciences. Common mix-up: psychology has "science" in its methods but it's not a Natural Science here.
    Question 3 of 5
    Where does College Algebra fit?
    College Algebra is Quantitative Reasoning (QR). So are Statistics, Finite Math, and most named "math" courses at this level.
    Question 4 of 5
    Where does Introduction to Philosophy fit?
    Philosophy is part of the Arts & Humanities tradition — it shares a category with history, literature, art, and music.
    Question 5 of 5
    Where does Biology with Lab fit?
    Biology with Lab is Natural Sciences. The "with Lab" part is the SL code — the hands-on requirement built into this category.
    Section 6 · Career Alignment

    Five careers. Five foundations.

    When a student picks a career path, every Gen Ed Core category contributes — but one usually does the most heavy lifting. Match each career to the category that gives the strongest foundation.

    📋
    A note before you start Every category matters for every career. The point of this activity isn't to rank them — it's to notice the strongest fit so you can help students see why a Gen Ed class isn't just a box to check.
    Question 1 of 5
    A student wants to become a nurse. Which category is the foundation?
    All five matter for nursing — communication for patient care, math for dosages, psychology for behavior, humanities for ethics. But Natural Sciences is the foundation. Bio and chem set up Anatomy & Physiology, Microbiology, and the rest of the nursing prerequisites.
    Question 2 of 5
    A student wants to start a small business. Which category does the most work?
    Communication and social sciences are huge for any entrepreneur — selling, hiring, managing people. But the daily reality of a small business is numbers: pricing, taxes, cash flow, payroll, margins. Quantitative Reasoning is the foundation that keeps the business alive.
    Question 3 of 5
    A student wants to be an elementary school teacher. Where do they spend the most time?
    Teaching is built on understanding how children grow, learn, and behave. That puts Social & Behavioral Sciences at the heart of teacher prep — developmental psychology, educational psychology, and child development all live in this category.
    Question 4 of 5
    A student wants to be a journalist or content creator. Which category is the foundation?
    Strong writing is the heart of journalism and content work, and Communication gives students the writing reps. Arts & humanities builds the analytical depth, and social sciences give them the context — but it starts with writing.
    Question 5 of 5
    A student dreams of being a historian or museum curator. Which category is the foundation?
    Arts & Humanities is home to history, literature, philosophy, and the methods used to study them. Museums, historical societies, publishing houses, and cultural institutions all run on humanities graduates.
    Section 7 · Dual Credit

    Gen Ed Core meets dual credit.

    Dual credit just means a college class a student takes while they're still in high school. They earn high school credit and college credit at the same time. And when that class is part of the Gen Ed Core, it also counts everywhere in Kentucky — not just at the school where they earned it.

    Say a student takes College Algebra through dual credit at their local KCTCS campus and gets the QR code on their transcript. Every Kentucky public university — UK, UofL, WKU, Murray, all of them — will count that class exactly the same as if the student had taken it on their own campus.

    That means a student's dual credit choices don't lock them into one school. The credit travels.

    Who benefits most

    Dual credit through the Gen Ed Core is especially valuable for three kinds of students:

    U
    The undecided student
    Still figuring out the plan

    They're not sure where they'll go, what they'll major in, or whether they'll start at a two-year or a four-year school. Gen Ed Core dual credit gives them a foundation that works anywhere — no doors close while they're deciding.

    T
    The future transfer
    Planning to move at some point

    They might start at a community college and finish at a university, or start in one program and switch. Gen Ed Core travels with them — they don't end up retaking what they've already done.

    W
    The student weighing options
    Comparing schools and programs

    They're looking at two or three Kentucky schools and trying to figure out which is the best fit. Gen Ed Core dual credit keeps every option open while they tour campuses and run the numbers.

    📜
    The policy is explicit about this The Gen Ed Core agreement specifically covers dual credit: when a student earns college credit while still in high school, those classes get checked against the same skills — and they transfer the same way as any other Gen Ed Core class.

    Section 8 · Advising

    Three questions, every conversation.

    When a student asks you about a class — any class, at any time — these three questions are the framework. Walking through them out loud with the student is half the work.

    1
    Will this credit transfer?

    If the student already knows where they're going (or thinks they do), the question is whether the credit will land at that destination. For Kentucky public schools, the Gen Ed Core gives you a clear answer.

    2
    Will it meet a requirement?

    Even if a class transfers, it needs to count for something. Will it satisfy a Gen Ed Core category? Will it knock out a major prerequisite? Or is it elective credit that doesn't move them closer to a degree?

    3
    Does it fit the career goal?

    Beyond requirements, does the class build a skill or background that matters for where the student wants to go? Sometimes the answer is "yes, indirectly" — and that's worth saying out loud, too.

    How to read a transcript

    Once you know what to look for, a Kentucky public college transcript tells you almost everything you need in three glances:

    Sample transcript · Jordan, year 1
    ENG 101 · English Composition I WC3 hrs
    ENG 102 · English Composition II WC3 hrs
    COM 181 · Public Speaking OC3 hrs
    MAT 150 · College Algebra QR3 hrs
    BIO 137 · Anatomy & Physiology I NS · SL4 hrs
    PSY 110 · Intro to Psychology SB3 hrs
    HIS 108 · History of the U.S. through 1865 AH3 hrs
    22 credit hours total Category Certified · 4 of 5 categories

    Three glances. First, scan the codes on the right — that tells you which categories the student has touched. Second, look at the credit hours — has the student hit the minimum each category needs? Third, look at the status line — that's the certification level we covered back in Section 2.

    Jordan above is Category Certified in four of five categories — Communication, QR, NS, SB, and AH all have at least one class. The one missing category? Look closer — Jordan has English Comp I and II (both WC) but no second SB or AH course yet. That's the next conversation.

    Section 10 · Final Check

    Final check: what stuck?

    Five quick review questions covering the key concepts from the module. Same rules as before — pick carefully, you can't change your answer.

    Question 1 of 5 · true or false
    The Gen Ed Core is an agreement among twenty-four Kentucky public colleges and universities.
    True. EKU, KSU, Morehead State, Murray State, NKU, UK, UofL, and WKU — plus the 16 KCTCS community and technical colleges — all participate.
    Question 2 of 5 · true or false
    If a student is Category Certified in three of the five categories, the other two categories will transfer to their new school as well.
    False. Only categories a student has actually finished will transfer. The two unfinished categories will need to be completed at the new school.
    Question 3 of 5 · true or false
    Dual credit classes taken in high school can count toward the Gen Ed Core, as long as they teach the same skills.
    True. The Gen Ed Core policy specifically covers dual credit. Those classes get checked against the same skills, and they transfer the same way as any other Gen Ed Core class.
    Question 4 of 5 · multiple choice
    To be Core Certified, a student needs to have...
    All five categories, 30+ credits, no double-counting. Option A is Category Certified, and option C is Fully Certified — both real, both different from Core Certified.
    Question 5 of 5 · multiple choice
    A student graduates from a KCTCS college with an approved AA transfer degree. When they transfer to a Kentucky public university, they enter as...
    Junior standing, general education fully complete. This is the guarantee that makes the KCTCS-to-university pathway work as smoothly as it does.
